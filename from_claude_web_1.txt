Here's what Minamino & Tateno (2014) actually found — it's more nuanced than a simple trunk-to-tip gradient, and interestingly species-dependent:

### What they measured

They defined the **daughter/mother ratio** = (total cross-sectional area of daughter branches) / (cross-sectional area of mother branch). Da Vinci's rule predicts this ratio = 1.0 everywhere.

### Key findings

**Two species, two different stories:**

| | **Fagus crenata** (beech) | **Abies homolepis** (fir) |
|---|---|---|
| Branching | Always 2 daughters | 2–5 daughters |
| Best fit | **Uniform stress model** (biomechanical) | **Da Vinci's rule** (area-preserving) |
| Interpretation | Mechanics dominate — branch shape is constrained by gravity/wind stress uniformity | Mechanical constraints less important; branching geometry follows simpler area conservation |

**The critical variable isn't branch order per se — it's the weight and angle of lateral daughters:**

- Both biomechanical models agreed with da Vinci's rule when the branching angles and weights of lateral daughter branches were small, but deviated as the weights and/or branching angles increased.
- The deviation was small for real branching patterns in nature, so da Vinci's rule did not critically contradict the biomechanical models in realistic conditions.

### So what about the Δ = 2 → 3 gradient?

Minamino & Tateno (2014) **didn't directly test the exponent gradient from trunk to tips**. Their paper is specifically about whether da Vinci's rule (Δ ≈ 2) is compatible with biomechanical stress models at individual branching nodes. Their conclusion is more subtle:

- At **heavy, wide-angled forks** (typical of large structural branches), biomechanics pushes the ratio *away* from 1.0 — the mother branch needs to be **thicker** than da Vinci predicts
- At **light, narrow-angled forks** (typical of small distal branches), da Vinci's rule holds fine

The trunk-to-tip Δ = 2 → 3 gradient you're thinking of comes more directly from **McCulloh et al. (2003)** and **Price et al. (2013)**, who showed that xylem conduits follow Murray's law (Δ → 3) when they don't need to provide structural support, but shift toward area-preserving (Δ → 2) where they do. Minamino & Tateno's contribution is showing **why** this happens mechanically — the stress equations demand it at heavy branch junctions.

### The key studies

**1. Bentley, Stegen, Savage, Smith et al. (2013)** — *Ecology Letters*
Measured branch radii and lengths across 5 species (maple, oak, piñon pine, ponderosa pine, balsa):

- Branching is area-preserving and statistically self-similar within trees. However, scaling exponents estimated for branch **length change across branching orders**, and exponents for scaling metabolic rate significantly differ from theoretical predictions.
- Variability in the scaling of branch radii is **less than** for branch lengths — meaning radius scaling (the Δ exponent) is relatively stable, but length scaling shifts with branch order.

**Key takeaway:** They found area-preservation (Δ ≈ 2) holds broadly across orders for *external* branch diameters, but the length exponent *b* is where the order-dependent variation shows up. The exponent gradient isn't cleanly visible in the external area ratio alone.

---

**2. Price, Knox & Brodribb (2013)** — *PLOS ONE* (349 leaf vein networks)

This is the strongest direct evidence for the gradient, but at the **vein** scale inside leaves:

- As veins increased in size, the area ratios shifted systematically toward values predicted under area-preserving branching, while the smaller veins followed a Murray's law pattern.
- Leaf venation achieves an optimal solution by scaling large mass-bearing ranks consistent with a biomechanical optimum, while the smaller veins involved in distributing water follow a Murray's law pattern.

---

**3. Price, Dahle & Grabosky (2025)** — *bioRxiv* (Flow Similarity Model, *Acer platanoides*)

This is the most recent and most directly relevant paper. They measured 3,484 individual stem internodes across 4 maple trees and explicitly tested for order-dependent exponent shifts:

- The flow similarity model argues that the physiological constraints experienced by plants change as a function of branch order and size, with more **basal portions satisfying biomechanical constraints**, and more **distal portions, hydraulic ones**.
- As biomechanical demands become large, the flow similarity model predicts that scaling relationships for the **basal parts** of the tree network will shift, trending toward slope values closer to those predicted by the **elastic similarity model**.
- The simplifying assumptions of the flow similarity model are most closely met when plant parts are governed more by **hydraulics than biomechanical demands**, as observed in **small plants or the distal portions of large plants**. As plants increase in size, the demands of self-loading drive scaling toward elastic similarity.

Measured the radius scaling exponent α across real trees and artistic depictions:
- Artistic depictions of trees show α values from **1.5 to 2.8**, similar to natural trees.
- They found the exponent varies but treated whole trees; they didn't decompose by branch order.

| Scale | Gradient observed? | Source |
|-------|-------------------|--------|
| **Leaf veins** (small → large orders) | **Yes, clearly** | Price et al. 2013 |
| **Xylem conduits** (twigs vs structural wood) | **Yes** | McCulloh et al. 2003 |

The flow similarity model (Price et al. 2022, 2025) is the closest framework that explicitly predicts and tests this transition, and their *Acer* data support it, but the field is still catching up to measuring this comprehensively across species.



Ok so for the sake of external structure is an exponent of about 2 a good enough approximation? Or should we model a variable exponent based on some other factors?



For **external branch geometry**, Δ ≈ 2 is a solid default. Here's why, and when you might want more:

## Δ = 2 is good enough when:

- You care about **visual/structural realism** (crown shape, branch proportions)
- You're modeling **mechanical behavior** (wind load, weight distribution)
- Your model doesn't resolve internal vasculature
- You want a **single-parameter model** that's simple and well-supported

The empirical range for external branching is roughly **1.8–2.3** across species, so Δ = 2 sits right at the central tendency.

## When you'd want a variable exponent:

Only if your model needs to capture **functional differences** between branch orders — specifically:

| Factor | Effect on Δ | When it matters |
|--------|------------|-----------------|
| Branch weight/angle | Heavy laterals → Δ slightly > 2 (mother must be thicker) | Spreading crowns (oaks) |
| Branch shedding | Shed daughters → Δ < 2 at that node | Modeling crown thinning/aging |
| Sapwood vs heartwood | Functional sapwood follows Murray (~3); total cross-section follows Leonardo (~2) | If you're modeling sap flow |
| Species habit | Conifers (excurrent) vs broadleaves (decurrent) shift Δ slightly | Multi-species forests |

## My recommendation

Use a **two-tier approach**:

```
External geometry:     Δ = 2       (fixed, for structure)
Internal conductance:  α = 3       (if/when you model water transport)
```

This is essentially what the literature supports — the two exponents govern **different things** (wood cross-section vs. xylem conduit radii), and they coexist within the same tree. The "gradient" from 2→3 isn't really Δ changing along the tree — it's that **which optimization matters more** shifts from mechanical to hydraulic as branches get small. But the *external* area ratio stays close to 2 throughout.

If you want one refinement without full complexity, make Δ **species-tunable** in the range [1.8, 2.3] rather than order-dependent. That captures more real variation than a gradient would.
